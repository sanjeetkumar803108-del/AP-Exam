FRQ 3: Topic 8.2: Energy Flow Through Ecosystems - Allometric Metabolic Rate Scaling and Thermoregulation Official Model Solution

★★★★★ VERY IMPORTANT
STEP-BY-STEP ANALYTICAL SOLUTION
Part ( (A)): • Surface Area-to-Volume Mechanism:
- Small animals have a substantially higher surface area-to-volume ratio (SA/V = 1.45 cm⁻¹ in mouse vs. 0.04 cm⁻¹ in horse).
- Because body heat exchange with the environment occurs across the body surface, a small endotherm loses heat to cooler ambient air at an extremely high rate per unit of tissue.
- To maintain a constant core body temperature (~37°C), the mouse must burn metabolic fuel at a dramatically elevated rate per gram of tissue, yielding a 16-fold higher mass-specific BMR than the horse.
Part ( (B)): • Heart Rate and Metabolic Scaling:
- High mass-specific oxygen consumption requires rapid delivery of oxygenated blood to somatic tissues. The heart must beat faster (650 bpm in mouse vs. 35 bpm in horse) to generate high cardiac output per unit mass.
• Microvascular and Cellular Differences:
- Small mammals have significantly higher capillary density per square millimeter of skeletal muscle, shortening oxygen diffusion distances.
- Skeletal muscle cells in small mammals possess significantly higher mitochondrial volume density and higher concentrations of respiratory chain enzymes (cytochrome c oxidase) to support rapid ATP synthesis.
Part ( (C)): • Daily Oxygen Consumption:
- Mouse: 0.016 L O₂/hr × 24 hr/day = 0.384 L O₂/day.
- Horse: 50.0 L O₂/hr × 24 hr/day = 1,200 L O₂/day.
• Food Intake as Percentage of Body Weight:
- Harvest mouse must consume approximately 50% to 100% of its body mass daily in high-calorie food (seeds/insects) to offset rapid heat dissipation.
- A horse consumes only about 2% to 3% of its body weight daily in forage, reflecting its lower mass-specific metabolic demand.
Part ( (D)): • Prediction:
- Endothermic Mouse: Mass-specific metabolic rate will increase sharply.
- Ectothermic Lizard: Mass-specific metabolic rate will decrease sharply.
• Justification:
- Endotherms maintain homeothermy; colder air increases the thermal gradient (ΔT), requiring shivering thermogenesis and elevated cellular respiration to generate heat.
- Ectotherms derive heat from the environment; in cold air, their core body temperature drops, slowing kinetic molecular collisions and enzymatic reaction rates (Q₁₀ effect), resulting in depressed cellular respiration.

Part ( (B)): • Decline in Per Capita Growth Rate: As density rises, intraspecific competition for food and nesting sites intensifies. Each individual receives fewer resources, leading to reduced per capita fecundity and increased per capita mortality; thus, the per capita rate r = r_max × [(K - N)/K] declines linearly with N.
• Unimodal Absolute Growth Rate (dN/dt):
- Total population growth is the product of breeding population size (N) multiplied by per capita rate.
- When N is small (N = 100), per capita rate is high (0.54), but few breeding individuals exist, so total recruitment is low (54).
- When N is near K (N = 950), many breeders exist, but per capita recruitment is near zero (0.03), so total additions are low (28.5).
- The maximum recruitment occurs at the inflection point (N = K/2 = 500), where the product of breeding adults and per capita growth is maximized (150).
Part ( (C)): • Density-Dependent Limiting Factors (any two):
1. Food resource competition: As pheasant numbers rise, high consumption of weed seeds and insects depletes food supplies, leading to malnutrition, reduced egg clutch size, and juvenile starvation.
2. Nesting cover / Territorial space: Competition for dense, sheltered brush for nesting increases. Subordinate females forced into open, marginal cover suffer elevated predation from raptors.
3. Parasite / Disease transmission: Crowding elevates contact rates and fecal contamination, facilitating the spread of avian coccidiosis or lice.
Part ( (D)): • Prediction: Carrying capacity (K) will decline (e.g., from 1,000 to ~600 pheasants).

• Resource Partitioning in Experiment 4:
1. Microhabitat partitioning: P. caudatum feeds exclusively in the upper, suspended water column, while P. bursaria feeds at the bottom on the glass substrate.

FRQ 10: Topic 8.7: Disruptions to Ecosystems - Anthropogenic Eutrophication and Hypoxic Dead Zones Official Model Solution

★★★★★ VERY IMPORTANT
STEP-BY-STEP ANALYTICAL SOLUTION
Part ( (A)): • Chronological Steps of Eutrophication:
1. Nutrient influx: Heavy spring rains wash synthetic nitrogen fertilizers (NO₃⁻) and phosphates from agricultural soils into waterways, discharging into the Gulf.
2. Algal bloom: High nutrient concentrations stimulate explosive mitotic division of photosynthetic phytoplankton (diatoms and cyanobacteria) in warm, sunlit surface waters.
3. Biomass senescence and sinking: Phytoplankton exhaust nutrients, die, and sink in massive quantities to the seabed.
4. Microbial respiration and oxygen depletion: Heterotrophic aerobic decomposer bacteria proliferate to digest the dead organic matter. Through aerobic cellular respiration, these trillions of bacteria consume dissolved oxygen, plunging bottom waters into severe hypoxia (<2.0 mg/L).
Part ( (B)): • Role of the Pycnocline:
- Fresh Mississippi river water is warm and low in salinity (24 psu), making it buoyant and light. Deep ocean water is cold and saline (36 psu), making it heavy and dense.
- The sharp density gradient between these layers creates a physical barrier called a pycnocline (density stratification).
- This stratification prevents vertical convective mixing. Dissolved oxygen produced at the surface by waves and photosynthesis cannot penetrate downward through the density barrier, leaving bottom waters isolated from re-oxygenation.
Part ( (C)): • Consequences on Organisms:
- Mobile organisms (fish, shrimp): Flee the hypoxic zone entirely (behavioral avoidance). This crowds commercial species into narrow coastal fringe habitats ('shrimp jubilees'), disrupting migratory patterns and increasing vulnerability to overfishing.
- Sessile organisms (bivalves, polychaete worms): Cannot flee. Prolonged exposure to DO < 2.0 mg/L halts aerobic mitochondrial ATP synthesis, causing cellular asphyxiation, tissue necrosis, and mass benthic mortality.
Part ( (D)): • Watershed Conservation Strategies (any two):
1. Riparian buffer strips: Planting deep-rooted native perennial grasses, shrubs, and trees along agricultural stream edges. Plant roots physically trap sediment-bound phosphorus, while rhizosphere denitrifying bacteria convert dissolved nitrate into harmless inert N₂ gas before it reaches waterways.
2. Winter cover crops: Planting non-cash cover crops (winter rye, clover) after autumn corn harvest. Cover crops absorb residual soil nitrates, preventing winter and spring rain leaching into tile drains.
3. Precision fertilizer management: Utilizing soil nutrient testing and GPS-guided precision application to apply fertilizer only at rates crops can immediately absorb, minimizing surplus runoff.
