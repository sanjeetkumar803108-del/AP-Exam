FRQ 9: FRQ 9: The Pasteur Effect and Metabolic Switching in Yeast Bioenergetics Official Model Solution

★★★★★ VERY IMPORTANT
STEP-BY-STEP ANALYTICAL SOLUTION
Part (a): In anaerobic conditions (Condition A), yeast consume glucose at an astonishing rate of 78.5 mmol/g·hr, which is 7 times faster than in aerobic conditions (Condition B, 11.2 mmol/g·hr). This dramatic suppression of glucose consumption and fermentation upon the introduction of oxygen is known as the 'Pasteur Effect.'
Part (b): The vast difference in glucose consumption reflects the stark thermodynamic efficiency disparity between fermentation and aerobic respiration: Under anaerobic conditions, yeast derive ATP strictly via substrate-level phosphorylation in glycolysis, producing a meager 2 moles of ATP per mole of glucose catabolized (an efficiency of ~2%). Under aerobic conditions, pyruvate enters the mitochondria and undergoes complete oxidation to CO2 and H2O through the Citric Acid Cycle and oxidative phosphorylation, yielding approximately 30 to 32 moles of ATP per glucose (~15 to 16 times higher yield per glucose). To sustain the same baseline ATP turnover rate, anaerobic yeast must catabolize 15 times more glucose molecules per unit time.
Part (c): Step 1: Pyruvate decarboxylase cleaves pyruvate (3C) into acetaldehyde (2C) and carbon dioxide gas (CO2). Step 2: Alcohol dehydrogenase reduces acetaldehyde to ethanol (2C), using NADH as the electron donor. This pathway produces zero ATP, but it is strictly essential because it oxidizes NADH back into NAD+. Without continuous regeneration of NAD+, step 6 of glycolysis (GAPDH) would stall, halting all ATP synthesis and killing the yeast.
Part (d): When oxygen is introduced, oxidative phosphorylation resumes in mitochondria, generating abundant ATP and citrate. High intracellular concentrations of ATP and citrate act as potent negative allosteric inhibitors of Phosphofructokinase-1 (PFK-1), the committed pacemaker enzyme of glycolysis. ATP binding to the allosteric regulatory site of PFK-1 lowers its affinity for fructose-6-phosphate, switching PFK-1 into the inactive T-state and rapidly throttling glycolytic flux down to match the cell's lower fuel requirements.

FRQ 10: FRQ 10: Molecular Variation in Photosynthetic Pigments and Ecological Fitness Official Model Solution

STEP-BY-STEP ANALYTICAL SOLUTION
Part (a): Chlorophyll a exhibits absorption peaks at 430 nm (blue) and 662 nm (red), while Chlorophyll b exhibits peaks shifted to 453 nm (blue) and 642 nm (orange-red). The single chemical substitution of a formyl group (−CHO) in place of a methyl group (−CH3) on ring II of Chlorophyll b alters the electronic resonance of the conjugated porphyrin ring. In shaded forest understories, the upper tree canopy absorbs most of the direct red (660 nm) and blue (430 nm) light; having abundant Chlorophyll b allows understory plants to absorb the remaining scattered wavelengths (450–500 nm and 640 nm), expanding their light-harvesting capacity and maximizing reproductive fitness in deep shade.
Part (b): Water molecules and dissolved organic compounds selectively absorb longer wavelengths of light (red, orange, and infrared) within the first 5 to 10 meters of the water column due to vibrational harmonic absorption of O–H bonds. In contrast, shorter green and blue-green wavelengths (480–550 nm) experience minimal absorption and penetrate deepest into benthic zones (up to 100+ meters in clear ocean). Green algae rely on Chlorophylls a and b, which absorb red and blue but reflect green; therefore, they are restricted to shallow surface waters (< 5 m). Red algae possess phycoerythrin, an accessory phycobilin that intensely absorbs penetrating green light (495–565 nm), allowing them to thrive in deep water where green algae starve.
Part (c): Accessory pigments like fucoxanthin and phycoerythrin do not perform primary photochemical electron transfer themselves. Instead, they are arranged in precise spatial proximity within light-harvesting protein complexes (such as phycobilisomes). When an accessory pigment absorbs a photon, it transfers its excitation energy down an energetic cascade to reaction center Chlorophyll a via Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET). This non-radiative dipole-dipole resonance transfers energy within picoseconds without losing physical electrons.
Part (d): Under high sediment turbidity, suspended clay and organic particles selectively scatter and attenuate penetrating green and blue light, while shallow waters receive predominantly red and orange light. Prediction: Green algae (which absorb red light via Chlorophyll a/b) will outcompete and displace red algae in shallow turbid water, leading to a decline in red algal abundance. Justification: Red algae rely on phycoerythrin to capture green light; when turbidity blocks green light, phycoerythrin becomes ineffective, whereas green algae can efficiently harvest the available red light.
