2. Herbivorous Sea Urchin: Trophic Level 2 — Primary Consumer (herbivore).
3. Sea Otter (Enhydra lutris): Trophic Level 3 — Secondary Consumer (carnivore/keystone predator).

• Part (b) Keystone Species Definition & Mechanism:
- Definition: A species whose ecological impact on community structure and biodiversity is disproportionately large relative to its numerical abundance or biomass.
- Mechanism: At Island A, sea otters feed voraciously on sea urchins, keeping urchin biomass low (12 g/m2). This allows kelp holdfasts to anchor and form dense, three-dimensional forest canopies (18 stipes/m2), providing critical nursery habitat, food, and structural shelter that supports high biodiversity (38 species).
• Part (c) Trophic Cascade Analysis:
- Consequences at Island B: With otters gone, urchin biomass surges 35-fold to 420 g/m2. Grazing urchins sever kelp holdfasts, clear-cutting the seafloor into a barren wasteland ('urchin barren', kelp drops to 0.1 stipes/m2), triggering an 84% crash in species richness (from 38 down to 6 species).
- Top-down demonstration: Predation changes at the apex tier (whales eating otters) cascade downward through alternate trophic levels—suppressing otters, releasing herbivores, and destroying primary producers—illustrating top-down predatory regulation.
• Part (d) Reintroduction Failure Reason:
- Ecological reason: Reintroducing otters to Island B without addressing killer whale prey-switching will result in released otters being immediately consumed by resident killer whales; orcas shifted to otters because their primary prey (seals, sea lions, baleen whales) were depleted. Until offshore whale and pinniped populations recover, predation pressure on otters remains unsustainably high.

FRQ 10: 1.2 & 1.4 Arctic Biome Capstone: Permafrost Thaw & Carbon Feedbacks Official Model Solution ★★★★★ VERY IMPORTANT
STEP-BY-STEP ANALYTICAL SOLUTION
• Part (a) Tundra Abiotic Characteristics & Tree Limitation:
- Physical characteristics: Extremely cold, sub-zero mean annual temperatures with brief, cool summers; low annual precipitation (<250 mm, polar desert conditions); presence of an impermeable permafrost layer below a thin active surface layer.
- Why trees cannot establish: Permafrost forms a solid subterranean frozen barrier that physically prevents deep root penetration; seasonal freezing and thawing (cryoturbation) snaps roots; waterlogged anoxic active layers in summer inhibit root cellular respiration; and extreme desiccating winter winds destroy exposed tree buds above snowpack.
• Part (b) Positive Climate Feedback Loop:
1. Initial forcing: Anthropogenic emissions cause tropospheric warming, which penetrates arctic soils.
2. Thawing: The permafrost active layer thaws to greater depths, exposing ancient, frozen organic matter.
3. Microbial respiration: Soil bacteria and fungi resume cellular metabolism, decomposing organic carbon and releasing massive volumes of carbon dioxide (CO2) and methane (CH4).
4. Amplification: Elevated atmospheric CO2 and CH4 absorb more outgoing terrestrial infrared radiation, increasing global radiative forcing and driving temperatures higher, which melts more permafrost in a destabilizing, self-reinforcing cycle.
• Part (c) Aerobic vs Anaerobic Emissions & Warming Hazard:
- (i) Well-drained soils: Aerobic heterotrophic bacteria utilize O2 as the terminal electron acceptor, decomposing organic matter to produce carbon dioxide (CO2).
- (ii) Waterlogged thermokarst sediments: Anoxic conditions force anaerobic methanogenic archaea to decompose organic matter via methanogenesis, producing methane (CH4).
- Hazard comparison: Methane (CH4) possesses a 20-year Global Warming Potential (GWP) roughly 84 times greater than CO2; therefore, anaerobic thermokarst lake emissions cause much more rapid, intense near-term atmospheric warming.
• Part (d) Mitigation & Conservation Strategies:
- International mitigation: Adopt and enforce stringent multilateral decarbonization agreements (e.g., Paris Agreement) to phase out fossil fuel combustion, accelerate renewable energy deployment, and stop black carbon (soot) emissions that lower Arctic ice and snow albedo.
- Regional wildlife conservation: Designate protected, interconnected migration corridors for caribou and muskoxen, and regulate oil and mineral exploration infrastructure to avoid thermokarst thaw zones and sensitive tundra calving grounds.

APPENDIX FORMULA REFERENCE & THEOREMS
Essential Unit 1 Rules & Equations
Core formulas, biogeochemical pathways, and environmental principles required for AP Environmental Science Unit 1.
  • Net Primary Productivity (NPP): NPP = GPP − R (Net Primary Productivity = Gross Primary Productivity minus autotrophic cellular respiration). Typical units: g C/m²/yr or kcal/m²/yr.
  • The 10% Ecological Efficiency Rule: Only approximately 10% of usable chemical energy transfers from one trophic level to the next; ~90% is dissipated as metabolic heat (Second Law of Thermodynamics) or lost in unconsumed biomass.
  • Nitrogen Cycle Sequence: Nitrogen Fixation (N₂ → NH₃/NH₄⁺ via bacteria or lightning) → Nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻ via Nitrosomonas & Nitrobacter) → Assimilation (NO₃⁻ into plant tissue proteins/DNA) → Ammonification (decomposers convert dead matter into NH₄⁺) → Denitrification (anaerobic Pseudomonas convert NO₃⁻ back into N₂O and N₂).
  • Carbon Sinks & Reservoirs: Major long-term sinks: marine sediments and limestone rock (CaCO₃), deep ocean dissolved inorganic carbon, fossil fuel reservoirs, and old-growth forest biomass.
  • Phosphorus Limitation: Because phosphorus has no gaseous atmospheric reservoir and dissolves slowly from mineral apatite rocks, it frequently serves as the primary limiting nutrient in freshwater aquatic ecosystems.
  • Hydrologic Water Budget: Precipitation = Evapotranspiration + Stream Runoff + Groundwater Infiltration/Percolation. Clearcutting dramatically reduces transpiration, elevating surface runoff and nutrient leaching.
