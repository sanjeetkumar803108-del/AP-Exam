Course Blueprint & Exam Structure

This master practice examination covers the entirety of Unit 7: Industrial and Economic Development Patterns and Processes as defined by the College Board AP Human Geography Curriculum. Every item is mapped directly to essential knowledge statements and verified for rigorous academic accuracy.

7.1 The Industrial Revolution

Hearth in Great Britain (coal, iron, steam engine, water access); diffusion to Western Europe and the United States; impacts on food production, demographic transitions, and colonial resource extraction.

7.2 Economic Sectors & Patterns

Primary (extractive), Secondary (manufacturing), Tertiary (services), Quaternary (knowledge/R&D), and Quinary (elite decisions); Alfred Weber's Least Cost Theory (bulk-reducing vs. bulk-gaining; transport, labor, agglomeration).

7.3 Measures of Development

Economic indicators (GDP, GNP, GNI per capita PPP, Gini coefficient, informal economy); UN Human Development Index (HDI: life expectancy, education, income); limitations of single per-capita metrics.

7.4 Women & Economic Development

Gender Inequality Index (GII: reproductive health, empowerment, labor force); impacts of female education and fertility decline; microloans/microfinance (Grameen Bank); barriers to property ownership and wage equity.

7.5 Theories of Development

Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth (5 sequential modernization stages, Eurocentric critique); Wallerstein's World Systems Theory (Core, Semi-Periphery, Periphery); Commodity dependence and Dutch Disease.

7.6 Trade & the World Economy

Complementarity and comparative advantage; Neoliberal trade liberalization; Supranational trade blocs (USMCA, EU); World Trade Organization (WTO); Structural Adjustment Programs (SAPs); supply chain fragility.

7.7 Changes from Global Economy

Deindustrialization in core manufacturing belts (Rust Belt, brownfields); Growth poles and technopoles (Research Triangle, Silicon Valley); New International Division of Labor (NIDL); SEZs (Shenzhen), EPZs, Maquiladoras; Just-In-Time (JIT) logistics.

7.8 Sustainable Development

UN 2030 Sustainable Development Goals (17 SDGs); Ecotourism benefits and critiques (greenwashing); Renewable energy spatial transitions; Environmental externalities; Circular vs. linear economies.

Unit 7 Economic Models & Industrial Systems Reference

High-Yield Summary of Industrial Location Theories, Modernization Models, World Systems Hierarchy, and Sustainable Development Indicators.

1. Alfred Weber's Least Cost Theory (1909)

  • Primary Variable: Minimization of total transportation costs.
  • Bulk-Reducing (Weight-Losing): Finished good weighs less or is less bulky than raw inputs (e.g., copper smelting, paper mills). Locates near raw material source (Material-Oriented).
  • Bulk-Gaining (Weight-Gaining): Finished product gains weight, volume, or fragility (e.g., soft drink bottling, commercial bakeries). Locates near consumer market (Market-Oriented).
  • Secondary Variables: Labor cost minimization and Agglomeration economies (external savings from clustering).

2. Rostow's Stages of Economic Growth (1960)

  • Stage 1 (Traditional Society): Subsistence agriculture, static pre-Newtonian technology, rigid clan hierarchy.
  • Stage 2 (Preconditions for Takeoff): Commercial exploitation of agriculture, infrastructure investment (canals, roads), progressive elite.
  • Stage 3 (Takeoff): Rapid growth in leading manufacturing sectors (textiles), capital investment > 10% of national income, rapid urbanization.
  • Stage 4 (Drive to Maturity): Diffusion of modern technology across diverse industries, skilled labor.
  • Stage 5 (High Mass Consumption): Consumer durables (cars, appliances), dominant tertiary/quaternary service economy.
  • Critique: Eurocentric, assumes linear development, ignores colonial resource extraction and dependency.

3. Wallerstein's World Systems Theory (1974)

  • Core: High-income, high-skill, capital-intensive technology, dominates global finance and trade (US, Western Europe, Japan). Exploits periphery for surplus.
  • Semi-Periphery: Industrializing, intermediate wages, diversified manufacturing, exploits periphery while exploited by core (China, Brazil, Mexico, India).
  • Periphery: Low-income, low-skill, primary commodity extraction (mining, cash crops), weak state institutions, structural dependence (much of Sub-Saharan Africa).
  • Commodity Dependence: Primary commodities >= 60% of merchandise exports (UNCTAD), extreme vulnerability to price shocks.

4. Development Metrics & Global Transformations

  • Human Development Index (HDI): Composite of Life Expectancy (health), Mean/Expected Schooling (education), and GNI Per Capita PPP (living standard).
  • Gender Inequality Index (GII): Composite of Reproductive Health (MMR/ABR), Empowerment (parliament/education), and Labor Force. 0 = equality, 1 = inequality.
  • New International Division of Labor (NIDL): Offshoring low-wage assembly to EPZs/SEZs/Maquiladoras while retaining R&D/quaternary design in core.
  • Just-in-Time (JIT) Delivery: Lean post-Fordist supply chains eliminating warehouse inventory; vulnerable to maritime choke points.
Key AP Human Geography Economic Terms:
• Brownfield: Contaminated abandoned post-industrial site • Growth Pole / Technopole: High-tech agglomeration cluster with regional multiplier effects
• Dutch Disease: Resource export boom appreciating currency and devastating domestic manufacturing • Comparative Advantage: Lower opportunity cost trade specialization
