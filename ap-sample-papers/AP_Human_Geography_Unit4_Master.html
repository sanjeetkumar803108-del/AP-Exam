9 Points Total
Official AP Model Response:
Part A: Identify International Treaty (1 pt):

Part B: Contrast Territorial Sea vs Exclusive Economic Zone (2 pts):
• Territorial Sea (12 nm): The coastal state exercises complete territorial sovereignty over the water column, seabed, and airspace overhead (foreign vessels have only innocent passage; no unauthorized overflight).
• Exclusive Economic Zone (200 nm): The coastal state has sovereign rights only over economic natural resources (fishing, drilling, mining), but does NOT control the airspace or surface navigation, meaning foreign ships and aircraft have full freedom of navigation.
Part C: Median-Line Principle and Application (2 pts):
• Definition: When the distance between two opposing littoral states is less than 400 nautical miles, their EEZs overlap, requiring the boundary to be drawn down the exact median line (equidistant from both baselines).
• Scenario: Applied in narrow waterways such as the North Sea between the UK and Norway, the Persian Gulf between Iran and Saudi Arabia, or the English Channel between Britain and France.
Part D: Two Economic Benefits of an EEZ (2 pts):
• Benefit 1: Exclusive commercial fishing rights, allowing the state to harvest marine protein or license quotas to foreign fishing fleets for revenue.
• Benefit 2: Exclusive rights to offshore subterranean petroleum, natural gas, and seabed mineral extraction (e.g., deep-sea drilling platforms in the Gulf of Mexico or North Sea).
Part E: Why China's Nine-Dash Line Conflicts with UNCLOS (1 pt):
• China claims historic rights covering roughly 90% of the South China Sea, which unlawfully overlaps with and encroaches upon the 200-nm EEZs of the Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia, and Brunei (ruled illegal by the Permanent Court of Arbitration in 2016).
Part F: Artificial Island Construction in Maritime Disputes (1 pt):
• States dredge sand over submerged coral reefs to construct military airstrips and radar hubs, attempting to project military power and unlawfully manufacture EEZ territorial claims around artificial land.

Part B: Contrast Antecedent vs Consequent Boundaries (2 pts):
• Antecedent boundary: Established before an area is significantly populated or before the cultural landscape developed (e.g., 49th parallel in 1818).
• Consequent boundary: Established after a dense population has settled, drawn deliberately to accommodate and separate existing cultural, religious, or ethnic divisions (e.g., India-Pakistan partition in 1947).
Part C: Manifestation of a Relict Boundary on the Landscape (2 pts):
• Even though the border is dismantled, it leaves tangible physical scars (graffiti-covered wall segments, former watchtowers converted to museums) and socio-economic imprints (disparities in highway quality, household wealth, and regional political voting patterns between former East and West Germany).
Part D: Allocational Boundary Dispute and Example (2 pts):
• Definition: A dispute over the division, access, and extraction of natural resources (oil, natural gas, river water, aquifers) that straddle a shared international border.
• Example: Iraq accusing Kuwait of slant-drilling the transboundary Rumaila oil field in 1990, or Egypt, Sudan, and Ethiopia disputing Nile River water allocation following Ethiopia's construction of the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam (GERD).
Part E: Define Operational Boundary Dispute (1 pt):
• A dispute over how a boundary should function, be managed, or be policed, typically centering on immigration policies, customs enforcement, smuggling interdiction, or refugee transit.
Part F: Challenge of Fluvial River Boundaries (1 pt):
• Rivers naturally change their physical course over time through flooding, bank erosion, and oxbow cutoff (avulsion), which alters the physical location of the border and creates conflicting territorial claims.

Electoral Geography: Reapportionment, Redistricting, and Gerrymandering
9 Points Total
Official AP Model Response:
Part A: Define Gerrymandering (1 pt):
• Gerrymandering is the political practice of drawing the boundaries of legislative voting districts to give an unfair electoral advantage to a particular political party, faction, or demographic group.
Part B: Sequence of Census, Reapportionment, and Redistricting (2 pts):
• 1. Census: Every 10 years, the federal government counts the entire resident population.
• 2. Reapportionment: The 435 seats in the US House of Representatives are mathematically reallocated among the 50 states based on relative population changes.
• 3. Redistricting: State governments redraw the internal geographic boundaries of congressional districts to ensure equal population per district.
Part C: Contrast Cracking vs Packing (2 pts):
• Cracking (Party A map): Dispersing Party B voters thinly across all five districts (leaving them at 40% everywhere) so their voting power is diluted and they fail to win a majority in any single district.
• Packing (Party B map): Concentrating Party A voters into two overwhelmingly packed districts (90% Party A), wasting their surplus votes and allowing Party B to win narrow 53% majorities in the other three districts.
Part D: Minority Winning a Majority (1 pt):
• Even though Party B received only 40% of the statewide vote, by packing Party A into two districts, Party B captures 3 out of 5 seats (a 60% legislative majority).
Part E: Two Negative Consequences of Gerrymandering (2 pts):
• Consequence 1: Creation of 'safe seats' where general elections are uncompetitive, leading to political polarization because incumbents only fear primary challengers from extreme ideological wings.
• Consequence 2: Voter disenfranchisement and apathy; voters whose ballots are diluted or packed feel their votes do not matter, resulting in depressed voter turnout.
Part F: How Independent Commissions Reform the Process (1 pt):
• They remove mapmaking power from partisan state legislators and assign it to non-partisan citizen panels legally mandated to prioritize geographic compactness, contiguity, and keeping municipal communities intact rather than protecting incumbents.

Part B: Two Reasons Why Japan Suits a Unitary System (2 pts):
• Reason 1: Geographic compactness and high infrastructure connectivity allow centralized ministries in Tokyo to efficiently administer public education, transport, and police nationwide.

Part C: Two Reasons Why India/Nigeria Suit a Federal System (2 pts):
• Reason 1: Vast geographic distances and diverse regional economies require local governments that understand and manage localized agriculture and infrastructure.

Part D: One Disadvantage of a Unitary System (1 pt):
• Hyper-centralization can cause the central government in a distant capital to ignore or misunderstand the unique economic and social needs of peripheral or minority regions.
Part E: One Disadvantage of a Federal System (1 pt):
• Conflicting or overlapping laws between national and provincial governments create legal redundancies, bureaucratic inefficiencies, and unequal civil rights or educational funding across provinces.
